## 1. One-Line Definition
A 2-minute STAR story about informally mentoring 4 junior-to-mid engineers in Angular, TypeScript, and API design at SAP Labs — through structured 1:1s, code review coaching, and pair programming — resulting in all four independently delivering features within 3 months.

## 3. Full STAR Script (2 minutes)

### Situation (12 seconds)
"At SAP Labs, our team grew from 6 to 10 engineers in six months — four new graduates and junior engineers joined. Their PRs were requiring 3 to 4 review cycles before merge, which was consuming a significant amount of senior engineering time and not moving the new engineers forward fast enough."

### Task (8 seconds)
"I didn't have a formal mentoring responsibility, but I could see that the pattern wasn't sustainable — headcount doubled but our team velocity barely moved. I decided to invest deliberately in getting all four junior engineers to independent delivery."

### Action (80 seconds)
"I started by spending one session with each of the four engineers to understand where their specific gaps were — it varied: one struggled with Angular's dependency injection patterns, one with how to design REST APIs, two with testing discipline.

For each person, I put together a 12-week learning roadmap — specific topics by week, one or two reference resources, and one existing codebase example in our repo they could study.

I set up weekly 30-minute sync calls with each person. These weren't status meetings — they were focused on one technical concept per session. If they'd reviewed a piece of code that week and hadn't understood it, we'd walk through it together.

In code reviews, I changed how I gave feedback. Instead of 'rework this,' I started writing 'here's what this pattern does, here's why it's a problem in our context, here's how I'd approach it differently — try this and explain back to me why it works.' That turned each review cycle into a teaching moment.

For their first independent features — ones I'd previously have taken myself — I assigned the task to them and did a pair programming session on day 1 to unblock the initial design, then stepped back. I was available to unblock but didn't guide day-to-day."

### Result (20 seconds)
"All four engineers delivered their first independent feature within 3 months. The average PR review cycle time for my mentees dropped from roughly 2 days to about 4 hours, because the first-draft quality improved significantly. My own time in reactive code review dropped, which gave me back bandwidth to work on the micro-frontend architecture initiative. The team velocity improvement was visible in the next 2 sprint retrospectives."

## 4. Follow-Up Questions & Answers

### Q1 — Depth on Mentoring Approach
**"How do you keep mentoring sustainable when you also have your own deliverables?"**

> The unsustainable version of mentoring is reactive: junior engineer gets stuck, messages me, I drop what I'm doing and help. That interrupts my focus and doesn't actually teach the engineer to get unstuck independently.
>
> The sustainable version has two parts. First, scheduled touchpoints: the 30-minute weekly sync replaces random interruptions. Most questions can wait for the weekly session. It trains engineers to batch their questions (good mental habit) and frees my deep work hours.
>
> Second — the teaching investment pays dividend over time. Each hour I spend explaining Angular dependency injection once, comprehensively, with the right mental model, means I save 5 hours of future PRs where the same misunderstanding creates rework. The 12-week investment of structured mentoring reduces my long-term review tax significantly.

### Q2 — Conflict or Difficulty
**"Was there any engineer where the mentoring didn't go as planned?"**

> One of the four had strong frontend ability but resisted my suggestions on API design — they pushed back in PRs with "this is how I was taught at university" framing. The tension was real.
>
> My response: I didn't use authority ("do it my way because I'm senior"). Instead, I asked them to write up both approaches as a short comparison — their version and my version — with the tradeoffs of each. When they wrote it down and had to defend both in writing, they saw the gap themselves. The decision became theirs rather than mine imposed.
>
> The lesson: engineers who push back on feedback are often the most capable — they care enough to argue. The mentoring work is creating the conditions for them to arrive at the better approach by their own reasoning, not by submission.

### Q3 — Growth Layer
**"What would you do differently?"**

> I'd write explicit mentoring goals with each person at week 1 — a short document: "By week 12, you should be able to own a feature from design doc to production deploy independently, including writing contract tests for your API." And I'd ask them to co-write it so they have agency in the definition.
>
> One mentee and I had different interpretations of "own API design." I expected RFC-level design documents. They thought it meant writing the implementation. We were 8 weeks in before I saw the gap and corrected it. A shared written expectation from week 1 would have aligned us immediately.
